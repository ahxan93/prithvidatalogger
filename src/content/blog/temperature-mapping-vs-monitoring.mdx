These two terms get used almost interchangeably, but they describe two different pieces of work — one that happens occasionally, and one that runs continuously. Understanding the difference matters, because skipping the first step can leave the second one watching the wrong spot.

## What Temperature Mapping Is

Temperature mapping is a study of how temperature varies across a space — a cold room, warehouse, deep freezer, refrigerator, insulated box or reefer vehicle — rather than an assumption that the whole space behaves the same everywhere. During a mapping study, multiple sensors are placed throughout the space at once and readings are compared to find the warmest and coldest points, how temperature changes near doors, vents or walls, and how the space recovers after a door opens or a defrost cycle runs.

The result is a report that shows exactly where the hot and cold spots are, and — critically — the right positions for permanent monitoring sensors going forward. Our [temperature mapping service](/services/temperature-mapping), delivered by our parent company VEGA Calibrations, covers exactly this: cold rooms, warehouses, deep freezers, refrigerators, insulated boxes and reefer vehicles.

Mapping is typically done once when a space is first set up, and again whenever something changes that could affect airflow or temperature distribution — new racking, a different door, a change in how the space is loaded, or a scheduled re-qualification.

### What a Mapping Study Actually Involves

In practice, a mapping study means placing multiple temperature sensors throughout the space at the same time — not one sensor moved around on different days, but several running together so every position is measured under the same conditions. The study runs for a period long enough to cover how the space is normally used, including the events most likely to cause a temperature swing: a door opening and closing, a defrost cycle, a delivery being loaded in, or the space simply running through a full day and night.

Once the readings are collected, they're compared to find the warmest and coldest points, how quickly temperature recovers after a disturbance, and whether any area consistently runs closer to the edge of the acceptable range than the rest of the space. The result is a report, not just raw numbers — one that identifies where ongoing monitoring sensors should go and why.

### Signs You Might Need a Mapping Study

A few situations are worth treating as a prompt to map (or re-map) a space: setting up a new cold room, freezer or storage area for the first time; changing how a space is used or loaded, which can alter airflow; expanding or reconfiguring racking; experiencing a temperature excursion you can't fully explain; or preparing for an audit or qualification where evidence of sensor placement will be expected. If your current monitoring sensor sits wherever seemed convenient rather than wherever a study showed it should, that's also worth revisiting.

## What Temperature Monitoring Is

Temperature monitoring is the ongoing part: data loggers placed in the space, recording continuously and alerting the right people the moment a reading leaves its limits. Where mapping is a study carried out over a defined period, monitoring is permanent — it runs every day, around the clock, for as long as the space is in use.

This is where our [data loggers](/products) come in: devices like [Nakshatra](/products/nakshatra) for a single point, or multi-channel loggers like [Dhruva](/products/dhruva) and [Veer](/products/veer) for several points at once, placed exactly where a mapping study (or your own knowledge of the space) says they should go, recording temperature and humidity and raising an alert if a reading goes out of range.

Monitoring also builds the record that mapping, by its nature, can't. A mapping study is a snapshot — thorough, but limited to the days it ran. Monitoring runs every day after that, which is what lets you show not just that a space was mapped correctly once, but that it has stayed within range continuously since.

## Why the Order Matters

Monitoring without mapping means guessing at sensor placement — often the middle of a room, which is rarely where temperature problems actually show up first. A corner near a door, a shelf below a vent, or the back of a deep freezer can all run warmer or colder than the centre of the space without anyone knowing, simply because no reading was ever taken there.

Consider a cold room with a single logger mounted in the centre, away from the door. Every day it reports a steady, comfortable reading well within range — but the pallets stacked closest to the door, where warm air enters every time someone goes in or out, could be running several degrees warmer without the central sensor ever showing it. A mapping study is what catches this kind of blind spot before it becomes a problem, rather than after a batch has already been affected.

A mapping study removes the guesswork. It tells you, with evidence, exactly where the sensor needs to sit to catch a problem early — and once that's established, continuous monitoring from that position gives you the ongoing record that a one-time study never could. Mapping is also something to revisit periodically, not just once: a space that was mapped correctly when it opened can behave differently years later if racking, usage or equipment has changed since.

## Do You Need Both?

For most regulated or temperature-sensitive storage — pharmaceutical stores, vaccine and cold chain facilities, food storage, laboratories — the honest answer is yes. Mapping tells you where to look; monitoring is what actually watches it every day after that. One without the other leaves a gap: mapping alone is a snapshot that goes stale the moment conditions change, and monitoring without mapping risks watching the wrong spot altogether.
