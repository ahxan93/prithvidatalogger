A data logger can record a temperature reading every minute, store a hundred thousand of them and send an alert the moment something goes wrong — and none of that means anything if the reading itself can't be trusted. That's what calibration is for, and it's worth understanding what it actually involves rather than treating it as a box to tick.

## What Calibration Actually Does

Calibration compares an instrument's readings against a reference of known accuracy and records the difference between them. In plain terms: you check what the logger says against something you already know is correct, and you write down how close — or how far off — it is. That comparison is what tells you how far each measurement from that instrument can be trusted.

This isn't a one-time property an instrument has forever. Sensors can drift gradually with use, age and the conditions they're exposed to, which is exactly why calibration is something you return to periodically rather than a step you complete once and forget.

## Why NABL Accreditation Specifically

Anyone can claim to calibrate an instrument. NABL (National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories) accreditation means the laboratory doing the calibration has itself been independently assessed against ISO/IEC 17025:2017, the international standard for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories. That assessment covers the laboratory's equipment, its procedures, its staff competence and its quality system — not just whether it owns the right reference instruments, but whether it uses them correctly and consistently.

## Why Traceability Is the Part That Actually Matters

A calibration certificate is only as good as what it's traceable to. "Traceable to national standards" means the reference instrument used for your calibration was itself checked against a higher-level reference, which was checked against another, in an unbroken chain that ultimately connects back to a recognised national measurement standard. That chain is what turns a calibration certificate from a piece of paper into actual evidence — it's what lets an auditor, a client or your own quality team trust the number on it.

Without that chain, a calibration is just one instrument being compared to another instrument of unknown accuracy, which tells you very little.

## How Often Should You Calibrate?

There's no single number that fits every instrument or every site — calibration intervals depend on how an instrument is used, the manufacturer's guidance, and what your own quality system requires. What matters more than any particular figure is having a defined schedule and following it consistently, so every instrument's calibration status is always known rather than assumed. A logger that's "probably fine" is a very different thing, from an audit standpoint, to one with a current, traceable certificate on file.

## What a Calibration Certificate Should Tell You

A proper calibration certificate is more than a signature confirming "this works." At minimum, it should identify the specific instrument calibrated, the date the calibration was performed, the reference standard it was checked against, the measured results at the points tested, and a statement of traceability showing that chain back to a national standard. Together, these are what let someone reviewing the certificate later — an auditor, a client, or your own team — understand exactly what was checked, against what, and how confident they can be in the result. A certificate missing any of these is harder to rely on, however official it looks.

## What This Means for Your Monitoring Data

Every temperature reading your data loggers record is only as reliable as the sensor that produced it. If a logger has drifted out of tolerance without anyone knowing, every reading since the drift started is questionable — including any alert decisions made from it. Regular, traceable calibration is what closes that gap: it's the evidence that the instrument producing your records was actually measuring correctly at the time.

This is also why we built calibration continuity into how our loggers work: if a device is away being calibrated, a substitute device covers the gap, and its data is merged back afterwards — so monitoring continues and the record stays complete, with no unmonitored period waiting on a calibration appointment.

## Calibration and Monitoring Work Together

It's worth being clear about what calibration is not: it isn't a replacement for monitoring, and monitoring isn't a replacement for it. Monitoring tells you what's happening in a space right now and keeps a continuous record of it. Calibration is what confirms the instrument producing that record is actually measuring correctly in the first place. A perfectly maintained monitoring programme built on an uncalibrated — or long-overdue — sensor is still building its record on an unverified foundation. The two need to run together: continuous monitoring for the ongoing picture, and periodic calibration for the evidence that the picture is accurate.
